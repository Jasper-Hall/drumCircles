# Canonical kick / snare / hat patterns — 13 electronic/dance genres

Methodology: four parallel research passes (WebSearch + WebFetch) across the 13 genres, plus a
targeted follow-up (below) that located and directly read an academic notated transcription for
the baile funk / tamborzão question the project owner specifically flagged. Patterns are reported
with confidence ratings; where no source actually *shows* a pattern (piano roll, step grid, staff
notation) rather than just describing the genre in prose, that is stated explicitly rather than
guessed. Position 1 = downbeat. All strings are exactly 16 characters.

---

## house   ~120–128 bpm

kick   `x...x...x...x...`    four-on-the-floor, kick on every quarter note (1,5,9,13)
snare  `....x.......x...`    clap (often layered with a snare/rim) on beats 2 & 4 (5,13) — the backbeat
hat    `..x...x...x...x.`    closed hat on the off-beat "and" of each beat (3,7,11,15); open hat frequently substituted here

variants: denser jackin'/tech-house hat — full 16ths with velocity accents on 3,7,11,15: `xxxxxxxxxxxxxxxx`
sources:
- https://kickdrum.io/patterns/four-on-the-floor — interactive step-sequencer preset; raw embedded step data gives kick 1/5/9/13, clap 5/13, hat 3/7/11/15
- https://www.attackmagazine.com/technique/beat-dissected/big-house/ — Beat Dissected breakdown tied to piano-roll screenshots: four-to-the-floor kick, clap on 2 & 4, off-beat hats with ghost hits
- https://www.musicradar.com/how-to/how-to-program-6-different-four-to-the-floor-grooves — independent confirmation of the same skeleton plus documented denser variants
confidence: high — three independent sources, one an actual programmed grid, converge exactly.

## reggaeton   ~90–100 bpm (felt half-time; also countable at ~180)

kick   `x..x....x..x....`    modern reggaeton: kick on beats 1 & 3, with a second/doubled hit anticipating the snare (1,4,9,12) — NOT four-on-the-floor
snare  `...x..x....x..x.`    the "boom-ch-boom-chick" dembow snare/clap at steps 4,7,12,15 — the pattern the project owner cited, now confirmed against an actual notated academic transcription (see sources)
hat    `x.x.x.x.x.x.x.x.`    steady 8th notes (some productions run a 16th-note shaker as ghost texture instead)

variants: the **original 1990 Steely & Clevie "Dem Bow" riddim** (reggaeton's direct ancestor) uses a plain four-on-the-floor kick `x...x...x...x...` under the *same* snare (4,7,12,15) — confirmed in the academic source below. A third source (kickdrum.io) gives snare/clap on 4,8,12,16 instead of 4,7,12,15 — flagged as a divergent, less-corroborated variant.
sources:
- https://wayneandwax.com/pdfs/dembow-paper-proofs.pdf — Wayne Marshall, "Dem Bow, Dembow, Dembo: Translation and Transnation in Reggaeton" (*Song and Popular Culture* 53, 2008). Figure 1/2 (p.135) is an actual box-notation transcription of the Steely & Clevie *Dem Bow* riddim: kick on every beat (1,5,9,13), snare at +3/+2/+3/+2 sixteenths from each downbeat = steps 4,7,12,15 exactly.
- https://bap.studio/grooves/dembow/ — contemporary production breakdown independently describing the modern doubled/anticipating kick (1,4,9,12) and the same snare positions (4,7,12,15)
- https://kickdrum.io/beats/reggaeton — interactive preset; diverges on snare (8,16) — reported as disagreement, not folded into the main pattern
confidence: medium-high for the snare (independently corroborated by an academic transcription and a production source); medium for the kick, since the original riddim and modern practice genuinely differ and both are legitimately sourced.

## dancehall (modern digital, generic "bubbling"-style)   ~90–110 bpm — riddim-dependent, no single canonical pattern for the genre as a whole

kick   `x..x....x..x....`    kick on beat 1 and the 4th sixteenth of beats 1 & 3 (1,4,9,12)
snare  `......x.......x.`    clap/snare on the "and" of beats 2 & 4 — a delayed backbeat (7,15), not house's 5/13
hat    `x.x.x.x.x.x.x.x.`    metronomic 8th-note hi-hat under the syncopated kick/clap

variants: rim/percussion ghost layer often added around the kick hits: `...x.x.....x.x..` (4,6,12,14)
sources:
- https://www.musicradar.com/how-to/how-to-program-dancehall-drum-patterns — explicit tutorial: kick on "first and fourth 16th-notes of beats 1 and 3," claps on "the 'and' of 2 and the 'and' of 4," metronomic 8th-note hats
- https://kickdrum.io/patterns/dancehall — interactive preset independently matching kick (1,4,9,12) and clap (7,15) placement, plus the rim layer
confidence: medium — dancehall is genuinely fragmented across dozens of riddims (Sleng Teng, Diwali, Fiesta, etc.); the pattern above is the one two independent sources converge on as the generic modern-digital template, not "the" dancehall beat.

Cross-genre note: the dancehall kick (1,4,9,12) and modern reggaeton kick (1,4,9,12) came out identical across independent sources — consistent with reggaeton's documented genealogical descent from the Jamaican Dem Bow riddim.

hat    dense, often shuffled/rolled 16ths — universally described as "frantic rolls and stutters," denser and more aggressive than reggaeton's hats, but no source gives exact on/off steps; represented here as `xxxxxxxxxxxxxxxx` with the caveat that real parts are rolled/accented, not flat

confidence: medium for the base kick/snare skeleton (two independent notated production sources); **low** specifically for what makes Dominican dembow's kick/snare *different* from reggaeton's — no source distinguishes them on the drum grid, only on tempo and hi-hat density/aggression.

**On the project owner's tresillo-percussion hypothesis:** neither confirmed nor refuted. No source found notates a separate tom/percussion tresillo layer for Dominican dembow specifically distinct from its kick or snare. Treat as an open question.

## bailefunk (Brazilian baile funk / funk carioca — tamborzão)   ~130–140 bpm

**This is the one genre where a real academic notated transcription was located, read directly, and verified by duration bookkeeping (all note+rest durations sum to exactly 16 sixteenth-notes). This significantly changes the picture from the initial hypothesis.**

kick   `x.....x...x.....`    bumbo (kick): hits at steps 1, 7, 11 — quarter note, eighth rest, quarter note, quarter note, eighth rest
snare  `..x...x.....x.x.`    **this is actually the CONGAS part, not a snare** — hits at steps 3, 7, 13, 15
hat    `x...x...x...x...`    **this is actually the TOM-TOMS part** — a steady quarter-note pulse (1,5,9,13), functioning as the driving pulse rather than a true hi-hat

**Critical finding:** the academically-transcribed "Tamborzão puro" (pure tamborzão) has **no independent snare/clap line at all** — it is a three-way interlock of kick (bumbo), toms, and congas. The commonly-cited producer-blog claim of a snare/clap at steps 4,7,12,14 (i.e., reggaeton's shape shifted one step early) could **not** be corroborated by this or any other source found — the actual notated congas part sits at steps 3,7,13,15, which is close to but not identical to that hypothesis (and not identical to reggaeton's 4,7,12,15 either). Modern baile funk productions commonly layer a clap/snare/gunshot sample on top of or in place of the congas — that layer is a producer convention, not part of the ethnomusicologically-documented "pure" tamborzão.

Also notable: several SEO/content-farm sources (beatkey.app and near-identical mirrors) claim tamborzão is fundamentally a 12-step **triplet** grid, not 16th notes. The actual academic transcription found contradicts this directly — its durations resolve cleanly to a standard 16-sixteenth 4/4 bar with no triplets required.

variants: producer convention — clap/snare/gunshot layered at roughly the reggaeton-dembow shape (≈4,7,12,15) is extremely common in modern tracks but is NOT part of the transcribed "pure" tamborzão above.
sources:
- Caceres, G.; Ferrari, L.; Palombini, C. "A era Lula/Tamborzão: política e sonoridade." *Revista do Instituto de Estudos Brasileiros*, Brasil, n.58, p.157–207, jun. 2014 — p.195, Figure captioned "De baixo para cima, o bumbo, os tom-tons e as congas do 'Tamborzão puro'" (bottom to top: the kick, the tom-toms, and the congas of "pure Tamborzão"). This is an actual staff-notation transcription (single-line rhythm staves for kick/toms/congas).
- https://anppom.org.br/anais/anaiscongresso_anppom_2018/5287/submission/director/5287-17986-1-DR.pdf — Moreira, R.H.M. & Valente, H.A.D., "Olha o beat envolvente: uma abordagem sonológica das batidas do funk paulista da terceira década," XXVIII Congresso ANPPOM, Manaus, 2018 — reproduces the Caceres/Ferrari/Palombini figure directly (their Figure 2, p.3), which I fetched, rendered at 500dpi, and read note-by-note, cross-checking durations against a full 16-sixteenth bar.
- https://splice.com/sounds/packs/splice/jlz-pack/story — prose-only background on the genre's three "foundational" percussive elements (kick/snare/percussion), used only for context, not notation.
confidence: **high** for the specific transcribed "Tamborzão puro" pattern above (a real, sourced, hand-verified academic score) — but flagged as representing the *classic/pure* form Palombini et al. analyze, not necessarily every modern baile funk track, many of which add their own snare/clap layer. Low confidence for any claim about what "the" snare does, since the pure form doesn't have one.

## cumbia (traditional acoustic pattern used as the best-sourced reference — see note)   ~90–110 bpm

**Note on scope:** no source was found that notates a pattern specific to *electronic* cumbia/cumbia villera. The best-documented rhythmic detail describes the traditional acoustic tambora/guacharaca pattern that digital cumbia and villera producers largely sample or replicate on a drum machine. Per the brief, this traditional pattern is used below and flagged as such.

kick   `x...x.x.x...x.x.`    tambora bass drum: "a primary quarter-note + two-eighth-notes pattern" per half-bar, repeated twice (steps 1,5,7 then 9,13,15)
snare  `..x...x...x...x.`    llamador ("weak pulse" drum) — sources say it "marks the weak pulses" without giving exact steps; positioned here on the off-8th steps as the most literal reading — low confidence
hat    `x.x.x.x.x.x.x.x.`    guacharaca: near-universally described as constant steady 8th notes ("chu-chucu-chu")

variants: some sources instead describe the underlying cumbia bass as a 3+3+2 tresillo/habanera figure: `x..x..x.x..x..x.` (1,4,7,9,12,15) rather than the tambora's quarter+2-eighths shape — both claims appear in different places and could not be reconciled, may reflect different cumbia sub-styles. For the specifically electronic/villera adaptation, one genre-database source instead describes a conventional dance-backbeat: kick `x...x...x...x...`, snare/clap `....x.......x...`, layered under the same guacharaca-style hat — the least-verified of the claims here (built on a single source that could be confirmed, plus one that could not be fetched).
sources:
- https://www.moderndrummer.com/article/september-2015-cumbia-drumset-adaptations-traditional-colombianpanamanian-rhythm/ — Modern Drummer (Steve Rucker & Carlomagno Araya): states the tambora plays "a primary quarter-note/two-8ths pattern" transferable to hi-hat/rim/woodblock — the single most concrete, citable rhythmic fact found for this genre, though only the free preview (not the full notated example) was accessible
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guacharaca plus independent corroboration on thecolombianway.com and cumbia.fm — consistent "steady 8th-note scrape" description repeated across unrelated sites
- https://www.melodigging.com/genre/digital-cumbia — source of the electronic/villera backbeat variant (BPM 85–110)
confidence: medium for the guacharaca 8th-note figure and tambora quarter+2-eighths shape (independently, consistently described by a specialist drumming publication and multiple other sites); low for exact snare/llamador placement and for anything claimed specific to electronic cumbia.

## tribal (Mexican tribal guarachero / 3ball)   ~128–150 bpm (Wikipedia's "140–280bpm" figure looks like a counting-convention outlier; 128–150 is what every other source and a concrete example track (133bpm) converge on)

**Structural note:** every technical source describes this rhythm as built on **triplets**, not straight 16th notes — the tom/conga interlock is better represented on a 12-step (eighth-note-triplet) grid than a 16-step one. The strings below are explicit 16-grid *approximations* of a triplet feel, not a faithful transcription — treat them as illustrative only.

kick   `x...x...x...x...`    steady four-on-the-floor kick — the one element every source agrees is NOT triplet-based
snare  `..x...x...x...x.`    *(triplet approximation, low confidence)* — represents syncopated low/mid toms and congas "cascading"/"tumbling" in a triplet feel; no source gives exact subdivisions
hat    `x.x.x.x.x.x.x.x.`    *(approximation, low confidence)* — guacharaca/güiro scrape layered in, described but never notated

variants: none found — sourcing was too thin to surface disagreement beyond the qualitative "triplet toms over 4/4 kick" description everyone repeats.
sources:
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tribal_guarachero — "the genre is often made up of cascading triplets" over a 4/4 beat
- https://xlr8r.com/features/what-is-it-tribal-guarachero/ — independently corroborates "a distinctive triplet rhythm... even as the songs hum along over a 4/4 beat at 133 beats per minute"
- https://www.melodigging.com/genre/tribal-guarachero — BPM 128–140 and instrument palette (kick, snare/clap, toms, congas/bongos, cowbells, timbales, guacharaca/güiro), same triplet-feel description
confidence: low for the exact percussion pattern (no notated source, and the triplet feel genuinely resists an honest 16-grid rendering); medium for the structural claim (steady 4/4 kick under triplet-feel percussion, 128–150bpm) since three independent sources converge on it.

## ukgarage (2-step specifically)   ~130–140 bpm

kick   `x.........x.....`    kick on beat 1 (step 1) and roughly halfway through beat 3 (step 11) — confirmed by two independent numeric tutorials; NOT on beat 3 itself (step 9)
snare  `....x.......x...`    snare/clap on beats 2 & 4 (5, 13) — a conventional backbeat position, confirmed by multiple sources despite how syncopated the kick is
hat    `..x...x...x...x.`    closed hat on the off-8ths (3,7,11,15) per Attack Magazine's explicit description; in practice most tutorials describe hats as hand-placed/dragged late or run denser with heavy swing rather than sitting on a fixed grid

variants: a commonly-repeated three-kick version, `x.....x...x.....` (kicks on 1,7,11) — this is the shape the project owner named — could not be traced to a dedicated notated source; the two clearest numeric sources found (MusicRadar, Orphiq) both describe only two kick hits (1 and 11). Swing settings cited across sources: 50–65% (various), MPC swing 68/69, SP1200-style 60%.
sources:
- https://www.attackmagazine.com/technique/beat-dissected/rolling-2-step-garage/ — notated Beat Dissected breakdown: snare "hitting on the second and fourth beats," hats "on every offbeat," swing 60–65%
- https://www.musicradar.com/how-to/uk-garage-tutorial — "a punchy kick on the first beat... and an 8th note after the third beat," claps on 2 & 4, swing 50–60%
- https://orphiq.com/resources/what-is-uk-garage — "kick on beat one, second kick halfway through beat three," snare/clap on 2 & 4, hats "pushed late"
confidence: high for kick (1,11) and snare (5,13) — three independent numeric sources agree; medium for the hi-hat grid, since most sources describe it qualitatively (swung/dragged) rather than as a fixed 16-step pattern. The user's hypothesized 3-kick variant (1,7,11) is plausible and circulates widely but was not found in a dedicated notated source in this pass.

## ukdrill (London drill)   ~136–142 bpm (half-time feel, often felt/counted around 68–71)

kick   `x.............x.`    illustrative single-bar example (steps 1 and 15) from a notated source — but the 808/kick genuinely varies bar-to-bar because it follows the sliding 808 bassline melody rather than a fixed rhythm; a second bar in the same source uses steps 1,4,7,15 instead. Treat this string as illustrative, not universal.
snare  `........x.......`    snare/clap on beat 3 (step 9) in the half-time feel — one source calls this placement "non-negotiable" for the genre, explicitly distinct from trap's standard backbeat
hat    `x..x..x.x..x..x.`    base pattern is a tresillo (3+3+2, doubled: steps 1,4,7,9,12,15), explicitly described as borrowed from grime ("two spaces, two spaces, one space, repeated"). On top of this, drill hats are very commonly layered with triplet rolls and stutter-fills that cannot be represented cleanly on a 16-step grid — sources consistently flag this as a defining, non-optional part of the genre's feel.

variants: alternate second-bar snare on beat 4 (step 13) instead of beat 3, used for call-and-response between bars, per Native Instruments' worked example; a ghost snare on the "and" of beat 4 (~step 15) at reduced velocity is also commonly added.
sources:
- https://blog.native-instruments.com/drill/ — most concrete source: "Add a hi-hat on the first, fourth and seventh 16th notes" (tresillo), "Put a snare on the third beat of the first bar, and another on the fourth beat of the second bar," explicit bar-to-bar kick/808 variation
- https://midimighty.com/blogs/resources/how-to-make-a-drill-beat-the-drum-pattern-behind-the-beat — independently confirms the grime-derived tresillo hi-hat description
- https://wavgrind.com/blogs/music-production/how-to-make-hard-hitting-drill-beats-step-by-step-guide — confirms half-time snare on beat 3 as "non-negotiable," stuttered triplet hats, and emphasizes 808 slides over any fixed kick grid
confidence: medium-high for snare placement and the hi-hat tresillo base (two independent sources agree closely); medium-low for kick/808, since sources agree the pattern is intentionally variable bar-to-bar rather than fixed.

## Summary of sourcing quality

**High confidence, notated sources found:** house; ukgarage (2-step) kick/snare; bailefunk/tamborzão (via direct academic transcription — see below).

**Medium confidence, corroborated but with genuine disagreement:** reggaeton (kick has two legitimate versions); dancehall (fragmented across riddims, but the generic pattern is well-corroborated); Dominican dembow (the base skeleton is solid, but nothing distinguishes it from reggaeton on the drum grid specifically); cumbia (traditional pattern reasonably well sourced; electronic-specific claim weak); ukdrill (snare/hat solid, kick intentionally variable).

**Low confidence / no notated source found:** kuduro; bouyon; tribal guarachero (plus explicitly triplet-based, poor 16-grid fit); ukfunky (sources disagree on the basic rhythmic foundation); hyphy (essentially undocumented as a distinct drum pattern in production literature).

**The one genre where the canonical pattern is NOT well expressed as a straight 16-step bar:** tribal guarachero (3ball) — every technical source describes it as triplet-based ("cascading triplets" over a steady 4/4 kick), better represented on a 12-step eighth-note-triplet grid. The 16-step string given is an explicit approximation.

**Correction to the project owner's stated priors:**
- Reggaeton dembow snare at steps 4,7,12,15 — **confirmed**, and now backed by an actual academic box-notation transcription (Wayne Marshall, 2008) in addition to production tutorials.
- Baile funk tamborzão snare at steps 4,7,12,14 (reggaeton's shape, shifted one step early) — **not corroborated**. The one real notated academic transcription found (Caceres/Ferrari/Palombini 2014) shows the "pure" tamborzão has no independent snare at all — it's a kick(1,7,11)/tom(1,5,9,13)/conga(3,7,13,15) interlock. The conga part is the closest analog to what people call "the tamborzão snare," and it sits at 3,7,13,15 — close to, but not the same as, either the original hypothesis or reggaeton's shape. Modern productions layering a reggaeton-style snare/clap/gunshot on top is a real, common producer convention, but it is layered *onto* the tamborzão, not part of its core.
- UK garage 2-step kick avoiding beat 3, commonly cited as hits on 1, 7, 11 — **partially confirmed**: the two clearest numeric sources found only show hits on 1 and 11 (not 7); the 3-hit (1,7,11) version circulates widely but wasn't traced to a dedicated notated source in this pass.
- Dembow Dominicano being faster/denser with a tresillo-based percussion layer, distinct from reggaeton's kick/snare — **not confirmed or refuted**. Every source distinguishes Dominican dembow from reggaeton only by tempo and hi-hat density/aggression; none isolates a distinct kick or snare/percussion tresillo layer specific to the Dominican variant.
